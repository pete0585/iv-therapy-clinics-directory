## Start With Your Own Criteria, Not a Ranking

Searching for an IV therapy clinic usually produces a long, flat list: names, addresses, a few services, maybe a map pin. That list is not a shortlist. A shortlist is a small set of options you have deliberately chosen because they match the way you actually want to receive care and the practical constraints of your life.

This guide focuses on the selection process itself. It does not evaluate clinical efficacy, does not recommend specific providers, and does not offer medical or legal advice. Any decision about whether IV therapy is appropriate for you belongs with a qualified professional who knows your health history.

What this guide can do is help you move from "too many tabs open" to "three or four options worth a closer look."

## Define What "Fits" Means Before You Compare

The most common shortlisting mistake is comparing clinics against each other before you have decided what matters to you. Two people can look at the same clinic page and reach opposite conclusions, because they are optimizing for different things.

Write down your own answers to these questions first:

- **Access:** How far are you willing to travel, and do you need evening or weekend availability?
- **Format:** Do you want an in-person visit, or are you open to a remote conversation as part of the process?
- **Setting:** Do you prefer a clinical environment, a spa-like environment, or something in between?
- **Communication:** How much explanation do you want before, during, and after a visit?
- **Follow-up:** What happens after the appointment — is there a plan for questions or next steps?
- **Budget framing:** What range are you comfortable exploring, understanding that you will confirm actual costs directly with each clinic?

You do not need perfect answers. You need enough of a draft that you can recognize a mismatch quickly.

## A Shortlisting Checklist You Can Reuse

Below is a checklist organized by stage. Treat it as a filter, not a scorecard. The goal is to eliminate poor fits early so you can spend your attention on the remaining options.

### Stage 1 — Broad filter

- [ ] Location is within your realistic travel range.
- [ ] Hours overlap with when you can actually attend.
- [ ] The clinic publicly describes the services it offers.
- [ ] There is a clear way to contact a human, not just a booking widget.
- [ ] The website answers basic questions without requiring a form submission first.

### Stage 2 — Fit filter

- [ ] The setting matches your preference (clinical, spa-like, or neutral).
- [ ] The intake process is described somewhere you can read it.
- [ ] The clinic explains how it handles questions before a visit.
- [ ] You can find information about who performs the service and how the visit is structured.
- [ ] The clinic is transparent about what it does *not* do.

### Stage 3 — Confidence filter

- [ ] You understand what a first visit would involve.
- [ ] You know how to ask about cost, and you know the answer will come directly from the clinic.
- [ ] You have at least one question prepared about follow-up.
- [ ] You can describe, in one sentence, why this clinic is still on your list.

That last item is the real test. If you cannot explain why an option survived, it probably should not have.

## Hypothetical Example: Two Different Shortlists

The following examples are invented for illustration only. They are not real clinics, not real people, and not recommendations.

**Example A — The commuter.** A person who works long hours wants a clinic near their office, open after 6 p.m., and able to accommodate a visit without a lengthy intake conversation beforehand. Their shortlist ends up with three options: two near the office and one near home as a backup. They eliminated several clinics that only operate during standard business hours, even though those clinics looked appealing in other ways. Their shortlist is small because their constraint is sharp.

**Example B — The researcher.** A person who prefers to read everything first wants a clinic that publishes detailed information about its intake process, its setting, and how visits are structured. They are willing to travel farther for a clinic that communicates thoroughly. Their shortlist includes two nearby options and one farther away, because communication quality outweighed distance for them.

Notice that neither example involves a claim about which clinic is "better." The shortlists differ because the people differ. That is the point of building a shortlist before comparing.

## What to Do When Two Options Look Identical

When two clinics appear equally matched on paper, the tiebreaker is usually a conversation, not more reading. A short, direct question can reveal more than an entire website.

Consider asking:

- "Can you walk me through what a first visit looks like, start to finish?"
- "Who will I actually be speaking with, and what is their role?"
- "If I have questions after the visit, how do I reach someone?"
- "What information do you need from me before a visit?"

You are not looking for a scripted answer. You are listening for whether the response is specific, calm, and willing to say "I don't know, let me find out." Vagueness is information too.

## Common Shortlisting Mistakes

A few patterns show up repeatedly when people narrow down IV therapy clinic options:

1. **Shortlisting by aesthetics alone.** A polished website is not a substitute for clear communication.
2. **Shortlisting by proximity alone.** Convenience matters, but so does whether the visit format suits you.
3. **Shortlisting too many options.** A list of twelve is not a shortlist. Aim for three to five.
4. **Skipping the follow-up question.** What happens after the appointment is part of the service, not an afterthought.
5. **Assuming cost is comparable without checking.** Costs vary, and the only reliable source is the clinic itself.

If you notice yourself avoiding a question because you are worried about the answer, that question probably belongs on your list.

## Turning a Shortlist Into a Decision

Once you have three to five options, the next step is not more research. It is a structured comparison using the criteria you wrote down at the start.

A simple approach:

1. Re-read your original criteria.
2. For each option, note where it clearly fits and where it clearly does not.
3. Remove any option with a hard mismatch — something you cannot work around.
4. Contact the remaining options with the same two or three questions.
5. Compare the responses, not just the websites.

This process will not produce a universally correct answer, because there is not one. It will produce a defensible choice that reflects your priorities, which is a more useful outcome.

## Before You Book: A Final Sanity Check

Before committing to a visit, confirm the practical details directly with the clinic:

- What the visit includes and how long it typically takes.
- What the current cost is, and whether anything is bundled or separate.
- What information they need from you in advance.
- Who to contact with questions afterward.

If you have health conditions, medications, or concerns about whether IV therapy is appropriate for you, raise those with a qualified healthcare professional. A clinic's website is not a substitute for individualized medical guidance.
